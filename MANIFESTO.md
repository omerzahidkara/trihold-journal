# Why I am making this game

*A personal manifesto for an independent RTS project still being built.*

I have wanted to build a real-time strategy game for years. The genre fascinates me because rules, incomplete information and competing priorities produce stories that nobody needs to script in advance.

A player decides to expand rather than defend. An Outpost falls, and the consequences reach the Mines behind it. A mobile force arrives a minute too late. A squad retreats instead of taking one more fight—and survives to matter again. These are the moments I want to create.

## Territory creates both opportunity and responsibility

Mines make territory economically meaningful. Outposts extend the space where players can build and give their frontiers defensive power. But an Outpost can be captured. A position you cannot defend is a different kind of investment from a position you can reliably hold.

## Reconnaissance should change the economy

Recon does not exist only to reveal enemies. Infantry can find field resources and earn Credits and Command Points, creating a competing investment against fixed Mines. I want players to consider the risk, the knowledge and the potential return—rather than treating scouting as an optional chore.

## The condition of an army matters

Numbers alone should not settle every fight. Fatigue, leadership, traits, injuries, morale and preparation can change the outcome. In manual development tests, six tired attackers have sometimes lost to a defending force of three that included a Sergeant with an advanced gun trait. That is a useful observation, not a universal rule or proof of finished balance.

An army also has a future after the battle. Wounded soldiers may be rescued, valuable transports preserved and hard-won territory reinforced. These choices should matter as much as immediate damage dealt.

## Technology should change the strategic problem

The current Base 1 environment is strongly shaped by Scout Cars and defensible, capturable Outposts. Later technology is intended to introduce specialized roles, stronger answers to vehicles and much more practical assaults on structures. I want a changing battlefield, not just inflated numbers.

## Complexity must become understandable

It is easy to build many interacting systems and difficult to communicate them. A player should be able to understand why a capture failed, why a squad is ineffective or why a Mine stopped paying. The visual language, UI, animation and feedback will ultimately determine whether the mechanics are readable.

## Progress is not proof of a finished game

I use AI-assisted software development as part of this work. It helped make the technical ambition feel possible, but no tool can replace design judgment, real playtests, maintainability or the responsibility to evaluate results. I want to show what is implemented, what was actually observed and what remains a hypothesis.

This journal is a way to introduce that process to players, fellow developers, artists and prospective collaborators—without pretending the project is already a finished game.

**If the game succeeds, it should succeed because the decisions are interesting—not because the feature list is long.**

— **Ömer Zahid Kara**
